Ionization explorer (EI vs CI)

Inside the ion source (1 cm across)
Electrons from the hot filament cross the source. In EI the pressure is so low (λ of tens of meters) that analyte molecules are hit by electrons and never collide with anything else; a 70 eV electron leaves several eV of extra energy in M⁺•, so it often breaks apart (purple fragments). In CI the source is filled with reagent gas at about 1 torr; electrons ionize the abundant reagent (orange), and after many collisions the reagent ions hand a proton to the analyte (blue to red-ringed). Only the energy difference PA(M) − PA(B) is left in MH⁺, so it fragments far less. Lower the pressure in CI mode and watch collisions, and CI, disappear.

What this tool shows

Before a mass spectrometer can measure anything, it has to turn neutral molecules into ions. How that happens decides what the spectrum looks like. This tool compares the two classic gas-phase methods: electron ionization (EI) and chemical ionization (CI).

Pressure and mean free path

The mean free path λ = kBT/(√2·π·d²·P) is the average distance a molecule travels between collisions. At EI pressures (about 10⁻⁶ torr) λ is tens of meters, so in a 1 cm source molecules essentially never collide. At CI pressures (about 1 torr) λ is tens of micrometers and every ion collides hundreds of times, which is what lets reagent ions react with the analyte.

Electron ionization: a hard method

A 70 eV electron removes an electron from the analyte (M + e⁻ → M⁺• + 2e⁻) and leaves several eV of excess energy, so M⁺• often breaks into fragments. The fragment pattern is a fingerprint used for library searches; the molecular ion may be weak or missing. Below the ionization energy, nothing ionizes; 70 eV is used because the yield has leveled off there, which makes spectra reproducible.

Chemical ionization: a soft method

Electrons ionize the reagent gas, which is present in huge excess. The reagent ions (CH₅⁺, t-C₄H₉⁺, NH₄⁺, H₃⁺) then transfer a proton to the analyte: BH⁺ + M → MH⁺ + B. The energy left in MH⁺ is the difference in proton affinities, PA(M) − PA(B). A reagent whose base has a low PA (H₂, CH₄) leaves a lot of energy and causes fragmentation; one with a high PA (isobutene, NH₃) is gentle and gives mostly [M+H]⁺. If PA(M) is below PA(B), proton transfer is uphill; with ammonia you then see the [M+NH₄]⁺ adduct instead. Argon works differently, by charge exchange (Ar⁺• + M → M⁺• + Ar), leaving RE − IE of energy, which is so much that spectra look EI-like.

Proton affinities and ionization energies are literature (NIST) values. EI spectra are simplified versions of the main library peaks. The fragmentation of MH⁺, the fraction of ions formed by CI at a given pressure, and the adduct intensities are simple models chosen to show the trends. The animation is not to scale.
